# SECTION 1: GLIOMA
# ------------------------------------------------------------

Glioma is a type of brain tumor that arises from glial cells, which are the supportive cells of the brain and spinal cord.
Gliomas are classified into low-grade (slow-growing, less aggressive) and high-grade (fast-growing, aggressive) types.
High-grade gliomas such as Glioblastoma Multiforme (GBM) are the most aggressive and have a poor prognosis.
Low-grade gliomas grow slowly and may not cause symptoms for years, but can eventually transform into high-grade tumors.
Glioma symptoms include persistent headaches, seizures, memory problems, personality changes, and vision or speech difficulties.
Treatment for glioma typically involves surgery to remove as much tumor as possible, followed by radiation therapy and chemotherapy.
Temozolomide is a standard chemotherapy drug used to treat glioblastoma after surgery and radiation.
Gliomas account for about 33% of all brain tumors and are more common in adults between 45 and 65 years old.
MRI scans are the primary imaging tool for detecting and monitoring gliomas due to their high soft tissue contrast.
Glioma recurrence is common even after aggressive treatment, making regular MRI follow-up essential for monitoring.
The median survival for glioblastoma (GBM) is approximately 14 to 16 months even with optimal treatment.
IDH mutation status is a key molecular marker in glioma classification and strongly influences prognosis.
IDH-mutant gliomas generally have a better prognosis compared to IDH-wildtype gliomas.
MGMT promoter methylation predicts better response to temozolomide chemotherapy in glioblastoma patients.
Bevacizumab (Avastin) is an anti-angiogenic drug sometimes used for recurrent glioblastoma to slow tumor blood vessel growth.
Gliomas can arise in any part of the brain but are most commonly found in the cerebral hemispheres in adults.
Pediatric gliomas often occur in the brainstem and cerebellum and may behave differently from adult gliomas.

# SECTION 2: MENINGIOMA
# ------------------------------------------------------------

Meningioma is a tumor that arises from the meninges, the three layers of membranes that surround the brain and spinal cord.
Most meningiomas are benign (non-cancerous) and grow very slowly, but some can be atypical or malignant.
Meningiomas are the most common primary brain tumor, accounting for about 37% of all brain tumors.
Women are more likely to develop meningiomas than men, especially during middle age, suggesting a hormonal influence.
Many meningiomas are discovered incidentally on brain scans done for other reasons and may not need immediate treatment.
Symptoms of meningioma depend on location and include headaches, vision problems, hearing loss, memory loss, and weakness in limbs.
Treatment options for meningioma include observation (watchful waiting), surgery, and radiation therapy.
Radiosurgery such as Gamma Knife is a non-invasive treatment option for small or surgically inaccessible meningiomas.
Meningiomas located near critical brain structures may be difficult to remove completely, increasing the risk of recurrence.
Exposure to ionizing radiation is a known risk factor for developing meningioma.
WHO Grade I meningiomas are benign with low recurrence risk; Grade II (atypical) and Grade III (malignant) carry higher recurrence rates.
Meningiomas can compress nearby brain tissue or nerves without invading them, which distinguishes them from malignant tumors.
Spinal meningiomas occur along the spinal cord and can cause back pain, weakness, or sensory changes in the limbs.
Long-term follow-up with periodic MRI is recommended even after successful meningioma surgery to detect any recurrence early.

# SECTION 3: PITUITARY TUMOR
# ------------------------------------------------------------

Pituitary tumors, also called pituitary adenomas, arise from the pituitary gland located at the base of the brain.
The pituitary gland controls many hormones including growth hormone, thyroid-stimulating hormone, and reproductive hormones.
Most pituitary tumors are benign and do not spread to other parts of the body.
Pituitary tumors are classified as functioning (hormone-secreting) or non-functioning (not secreting active hormones).
Functioning pituitary tumors can cause hormonal disorders such as Cushing's disease, acromegaly, or hyperprolactinemia.
Cushing's disease is caused by a pituitary tumor that produces excess ACTH, leading to dangerously high cortisol levels.
Acromegaly results from a pituitary tumor secreting excess growth hormone, causing abnormal growth of hands, feet, and face.
Hyperprolactinemia caused by a prolactinoma can lead to irregular menstrual cycles in women and reduced testosterone in men.
Symptoms of pituitary tumors include headaches, vision problems (especially loss of peripheral vision), fatigue, and hormonal imbalances.
Bitemporal hemianopia (loss of side vision in both eyes) is a classic sign of a pituitary tumor pressing on the optic chiasm.
Treatment for pituitary tumors includes medication, surgery through the nose (transsphenoidal surgery), and radiation therapy.
Dopamine agonists like cabergoline or bromocriptine are first-line medications for prolactin-secreting pituitary tumors.
Macroadenomas are pituitary tumors larger than 10mm and are more likely to cause visual symptoms due to compression of nearby structures.
Microadenomas are pituitary tumors smaller than 10mm, often found incidentally, and carry a better prognosis.
Pituitary apoplexy is a rare but serious emergency caused by sudden bleeding or infarction within a pituitary tumor.

# SECTION 4: NO TUMOR (NORMAL MRI)
# ------------------------------------------------------------

A normal brain MRI shows no evidence of tumor, abnormal masses, or suspicious lesions.
Even with a normal MRI result, patients should follow up with their doctor if neurological symptoms persist.
Normal MRI findings mean the AI model detected no signs of glioma, meningioma, or pituitary tumor in the scan.
A clear MRI scan is reassuring but does not rule out all neurological conditions — clinical evaluation remains important.
Some early-stage or very small tumors may not be visible even on high-quality MRI, so clinical correlation is always necessary.

# SECTION 5: GRAD-CAM EXPLAINABILITY
# ------------------------------------------------------------

Grad-CAM stands for Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping, a technique that visualizes which regions of an image influenced the AI model's decision.
In Grad-CAM heatmaps, red and yellow regions indicate areas the model focused on most heavily when making its prediction.
Blue and green regions in a Grad-CAM heatmap indicate areas the model paid little attention to during classification.
Grad-CAM helps doctors and patients understand why the AI made a specific classification, improving transparency and trust.
The Grad-CAM overlay shows the heatmap superimposed on the original MRI scan for easy visual comparison by clinicians.
Grad-CAM is generated from the last convolutional layer of the neural network, which captures the most high-level visual features.
If Grad-CAM highlights the center or a specific lobe of the brain, it suggests the suspected tumor may be located in that region.
Grad-CAM is an explainability tool — it does not replace a radiologist's interpretation but helps guide attention to suspicious areas.
When no tumor is detected, Grad-CAM is not generated because there is no class activation to visualize.
The NeuroVision system uses VGG16, a deep convolutional neural network, with Grad-CAM to provide visual explanations for its predictions.
Grad-CAM was introduced by Selvaraju et al. in 2017 and has since become a standard explainability method in medical AI systems.
Using Grad-CAM in clinical AI tools supports the principle of Explainable AI (XAI), which is critical for trust and regulatory compliance.

# SECTION 6: BRAIN MRI FUNDAMENTALS
# ------------------------------------------------------------

MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging) uses strong magnetic fields and radio waves to create detailed images of the brain.
Brain MRI is the gold standard imaging technique for detecting brain tumors due to its superior soft tissue contrast.
A contrast-enhanced MRI uses a gadolinium-based dye injected into the bloodstream to highlight abnormal areas more clearly.
MRI scans are non-invasive and do not use ionizing radiation, making them safe for repeated use over time.
T1-weighted MRI images are useful for seeing brain anatomy, while T2-weighted images better highlight fluid and abnormalities.
FLAIR (Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery) MRI sequences are particularly useful for detecting tumors and edema near the brain surface.
Brain tumors can appear as bright or dark spots on MRI depending on their type, water content, and contrast enhancement pattern.
MRI-guided biopsy allows surgeons to precisely sample tumor tissue from a specific region for laboratory pathological analysis.
Functional MRI (fMRI) maps brain activity and is used before surgery to identify critical brain regions to preserve.
Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) MRI tracks white matter fiber tracts and helps surgeons plan safe operative corridors through the brain.
CT scans are faster and more available than MRI but provide less soft tissue detail and are often used in emergency settings.
MRI spectroscopy (MRS) measures chemical metabolites in brain tissue and can help distinguish tumor from radiation necrosis.
Perfusion MRI measures blood flow in the brain and is useful for grading tumors and assessing treatment response.

# SECTION 7: SYMPTOMS AND GENERAL BRAIN TUMOR INFORMATION
# ------------------------------------------------------------

Common symptoms of brain tumors include persistent headaches (especially in the morning), seizures, nausea, and vomiting.
Cognitive symptoms of brain tumors can include memory loss, difficulty concentrating, and personality or behavior changes.
Motor symptoms such as weakness or numbness in arms or legs may indicate a brain tumor affecting motor pathways.
Vision problems including blurred vision, double vision, or loss of peripheral vision can be caused by brain tumors.
Speech difficulties such as slurred speech or trouble finding words (aphasia) can be signs of a brain tumor in language areas.
Brain tumor headaches are often worse in the morning, may wake patients from sleep, and worsen with coughing or bending over.
Not all brain tumors cause symptoms, especially slow-growing ones, and many are found incidentally during imaging for other reasons.
Risk factors for brain tumors include family history, prior exposure to ionizing radiation, and certain genetic syndromes.
Brain tumor diagnosis requires a combination of imaging (MRI/CT), neurological examination, and often a tissue biopsy for confirmation.
Survival rates for brain tumors vary widely depending on tumor type, grade, location, patient age, and overall health.
Seizures occur in approximately 30 to 50 percent of brain tumor patients and are often the first presenting symptom.
Increased intracranial pressure from a brain tumor can cause a triad of headache, vomiting, and papilledema (swelling of the optic disc).
Brain metastases (tumors that spread from other cancers) are actually more common than primary brain tumors in adults.
Lung, breast, melanoma, kidney, and colon cancers are the most common sources of brain metastases.
Corticosteroids such as dexamethasone are used to reduce brain swelling (cerebral edema) around brain tumors.

# SECTION 8: NEUROVISION AI SYSTEM INFORMATION
# ------------------------------------------------------------

The NeuroVision AI system classifies brain MRI scans into four categories: Glioma, Meningioma, Pituitary Tumor, and No Tumor.
NeuroVision uses a VGG16 deep convolutional neural network fine-tuned on a labeled dataset of brain MRI images.
The model was trained using supervised learning with MRI images labeled by category: glioma, meningioma, pituitary, and no tumor.
Data augmentation techniques such as rotation, flipping, and zoom were applied during training to improve model generalization.
The NeuroVision RAG pipeline retrieves the most relevant medical facts from this knowledge base to explain each AI prediction.
RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation, a technique that combines a retrieval system with a language model for grounded answers.
Sentence embeddings are used to find the most semantically similar knowledge facts to the user's query in the RAG pipeline.
The NeuroVision AI system is a decision-support tool and should never be used as a substitute for professional medical diagnosis.
Always consult a qualified neurologist or neurosurgeon for proper evaluation and treatment planning after any scan result.
The AI predictions made by NeuroVision carry a confidence score that reflects the model's certainty about its classification.
A lower confidence score means the model is less certain and the case should be prioritized for expert clinical review.
NeuroVision is designed to assist radiologists and clinicians, not to replace them, by flagging suspicious regions for review.
